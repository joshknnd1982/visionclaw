VisionClaw

Ask NVDA for anything in plain language, fully offline. Two small AI models on your own computer, a vision model and a coder model, break your request into small steps, agree on them, and carry them out one at a time. Nothing you do is sent to the internet. There are no accounts, keys or usage limits.

VisionClaw is based on VisionMate by Athiban Vijay and keeps everything VisionMate does. If VisionMate is installed, disable it in the Add-on Store: VisionClaw uses the same keys.

Every part of this page is a heading of level 2: in browse mode, press 2 or H to move from one part to the next. The first parts say what is new. Every feature of VisionClaw, old and new, is explained after them.

This help is for VisionClaw 1.16.0.

Getting started

After you install VisionClaw and restart NVDA, a window opens where you choose a vision model and a coder model. The suggested ones are the safe choice; every model is offered whatever your computer is. Press Enter to download them. While they download, press Space to hear the progress. An interrupted download continues from where it stopped. Press NVDA+Alt+M at any time to change your choice.

Then press NVDA+Alt+C, type what you want in your own words, and press Enter. You do not need to know NVDA: VisionClaw finds the right setting or command from your description and tells you the keys for next time.

The keys of VisionClaw

  • NVDA+Alt+C: ask VisionClaw. Pressed again while a request is done, it shows everything the last request did, in the Last task box.
  • NVDA+Alt+V: ask VisionClaw by voice; what it heard is read to you before anything is done.
  • NVDA+Alt+P: hear how far VisionClaw is with a request, a description, an answer about a picture, the words you spoke or the icons of the overlay.
  • NVDA+Alt+X: stop the request at once, or a description or answer about a picture that is still on its way.
  • NVDA+Alt+M: see, remove or swap the models on this computer.
  • NVDA+Alt+U: look for a newer version of VisionClaw.
  • NVDA+Alt+I: the skills window: read, make, change, turn off or delete skills.
  • NVDA+Alt+K: pointer mode: work the mouse pointer with the arrow keys.
  • NVDA+Alt+J: controller mode: work the mouse pointer with a connected game controller.
  • NVDA+Alt+A: describe the current element.
  • NVDA+Alt+W: describe the current window.
  • NVDA+Alt+F: describe the whole screen.
  • NVDA+Alt+D: describe the image, or copied image file, on the clipboard.
  • NVDA+Alt+O: open or close the overlay, the window as a list of its texts and icons.
  • NVDA+Alt+G: turn automatic descriptions of images without a label on or off.
  • NVDA+Alt+B: turn object names on or off: every object that is truly unlabelled is named by the local model wherever you move, in browse mode too.
  • NVDA+Shift+Alt+B: ask the local model to name the object where you are, also where object names left it alone.
  • NVDA+Alt+N: give your own name to the object where you are, or to an overlay item. Press it twice for the window of saved object names.

Every key can be changed in NVDA's Input Gestures dialog, under VisionClaw. Other things VisionClaw can do, such as opening the memories window, have no key; ask for them in words.

Asking VisionClaw

Press NVDA+Alt+C, type what you want, and press Enter. The window closes so the program you were in has the focus again, and VisionClaw tells you each step as it works. Some examples, each explained further down:

  • Make NVDA speak a little faster. Turn off the typing echo and set punctuation to all.
  • How do I read the title of this window? What does NVDA+T do? What does Windows+D do?
  • Open Notepad. Check and see if my recycle bin needs to be emptied. Turn down my system volume.
  • Create a folder called Taxes in my documents.
  • What is on my screen? Press the Play button in this window.
  • What is the focused control? What does this button do? Is this checkbox checked?
  • Explore this page. Why won't this form submit?
  • Write an add-on that beeps when a link gets the focus.
  • Remember that I use a braille display with 40 cells.

The two models must agree on how to handle your request before anything happens. If they do not agree, you are asked to choose. If you chose one model for both jobs, vision and code, there are not two to agree: the model chooses what to do and checks its own work, and VisionClaw says "the model". Its choice is checked against your own words; if the two still differ after it has looked again, you are asked which you meant, but only when one of the two ways would change something. When both only look, as describing the screen and looking it up in NVDA, VisionClaw does not ask: it tries one, what NVDA says before a picture, and the other if the first finds nothing, and tells you so.

If a step does not work, you are asked whether to skip it, try again or stop; a way that only looks and has another way waiting is followed by that way instead, without a question. A fault inside VisionClaw ends only the step it happened in; the rest of your request is still done. By default VisionClaw also asks before it changes a setting, clicks, presses keys or types; you can turn that off in the settings. Anything that could take speech or braille away, or that cannot be undone, is always asked about, whatever the settings say. What VisionClaw says it did is taken from what the tools really reported.

How VisionClaw understands your words

VisionClaw reads what you say the way a text adventure reads its player: some words are understood as other words that mean the same, before anything is decided. "Take a peek at the screen" is understood as "look at the screen", "kill the typing echo" as "turn off the typing echo", "give the Save button a click" as "click the Save button", "punch in control c" as "press control c", "google how to bake bread" as "search the web for how to bake bread", and "dump the bin" as "empty the recycle bin". Plain program code does this, from lines in a Markdown file that comes with VisionClaw, understanding.md in its claw folder; no model decides it and nothing goes online. When the meaning of a word was changed, the answer begins "Understood as". Words in quotes, text you ask to have typed, a name you give, what is to be searched for, web addresses, file names, keys, and every request to remember something, make a skill or teach a word are never changed. A way of saying what to do counts only where something to do begins: "fire up Notepad" opens Notepad, but "why does the fan fire up" is left as it is.

Teach VisionClaw your own words: say "when I say shh, I mean stop talking", "understand zap as turn off", or "by brb I mean lock the computer". VisionClaw asks you first, unless you turned questions off, and from then on that word is understood that way, before everything else. Ask "which words have I taught you" to hear them, "forget the word shh" to forget one, and "forget all the words I taught you" to forget all of them, which is always asked about first. They are kept in the file understanding.md in VisionClaw's data folder, one line each, which you can read and change in any editor. A word of fewer than two letters, an everyday word like "it", and a word that means itself cannot be taught; you are told why.

When VisionClaw got it wrong, say what you meant: "no, I meant turn off the typing echo", "not that, the notification area" or "that's not what I asked, I wanted the Start menu". VisionClaw does what you meant instead, and tells you which request it takes as corrected. When that went well, it asks once whether to understand your first words that way from now on. Say yes, and they are kept with your own words, so that the next time they are understood rightly at once; say no, and nothing is kept. It asks only when "Remember things" is on its first choice. To undo it, say "forget the word" and the words.

The add-on writer and questions about a picture understand your words too. After the whole request is understood, the part that writes an add-on reads it again with words of its own: "make an add-on that chimes whenever focus hits a link" is read as "beeps whenever focus lands on a link", and "yells 'new mail' when a dialog pops up" as "says 'new mail'". The part that asks about a picture does the same to know what kind of answer you want: "what's scribbled on the whiteboard" asks for what is written, "tally up the icons" for a count, "what's lit up" for what is highlighted; the model is still asked in your own words. Words in quotes, the words an add-on is to say, and keys are never changed. These words are in understanding.md, under headings that end ", in add-ons" and ", in questions about a picture". The description window answers your questions as before.

The work sheet: every request in small pieces

Every request has a work sheet: a Markdown file with the plan, what each small step is given, and what it found. Each question to a model is made from it, so that a small model gets only its own small piece of the work: a step is given what the steps of its own part found, not everything found so far, and a piece of an add-on is given its own section and nothing of the others. A step that speaks of "it" or "there", and a step a model planned itself, are given everything found before it. The work sheet is in the folder work of VisionClaw's data folder while the request runs, so you can open it to follow along. It is deleted when the request is done, also when it failed or you stopped it; one left over because NVDA closed in the middle of a request is deleted when VisionClaw starts.

While VisionClaw works: status updates

A large model, or a model set to think a lot first, can take a minute or more for one answer. So that you are never left wondering, VisionClaw tells you by itself how far it is when you have heard nothing from it for a while: "Still describing the screen, 30 seconds so far. The vision model is looking at the picture." The first update also tells you to press NVDA+Alt+P at any time to hear how far it is, and NVDA+Alt+X to stop it. This works for a request (NVDA+Alt+C or NVDA+Alt+V), a description (NVDA+Alt+A, W, F or D, and photos in chats), an answer to a question in the description window, the speech model working out what you said, the icons of the overlay being named (NVDA+Alt+O), and an add-on VisionClaw wrote being corrected.

An update says what VisionClaw is doing and what it waits for: which step of your request it is on; whether a model is still starting, which a large model takes a while to do; whether it is thinking before it answers, and at which level of thinking; or how many of the overlay's icons are done. When the icons took long enough for an update, you also hear when they are all done. Nothing is said while VisionClaw waits for your answer to a question, once the answer or description is being spoken or shown, or while the microphone is open.

Every step you already hear counts: the quiet starts again after each one, and after you press NVDA+Alt+P. To choose how long the quiet is, 10, 15, 30 or 60 seconds, or to turn the updates off, go to VisionClaw's settings: Status updates while VisionClaw takes long, as with a large model or high thinking. The suggested choice is 15 seconds. With the updates off, NVDA+Alt+P still says how far it is whenever you press it.

While VisionClaw waits for a description or an answer about a picture, other keys no longer stop it: you can read on, or press NVDA+Alt+P, and the description still comes. Press NVDA+Alt+X to stop it. Once it is being spoken, Control stops it, as before.

Answers spoken while they are written

When the answer to your request is written by a model, as when VisionClaw looked something up for you, you hear it a sentence at a time while the model is still writing it, instead of waiting in silence for all of it. Press NVDA+Alt+C and ask "how do I read the title of this window": the first sentence comes as soon as the model has written it. Descriptions (NVDA+Alt+A, W, F or D) and answers to questions in the description window are spoken the same way, and follow the same setting.

First comes how your request was understood, when VisionClaw says so, and what the tools did, which is ready at once; then the model's sentences as they come; then anything VisionClaw adds after it, such as a memory it kept. Keys are written as NVDA writes them in every sentence: NVDA+t, not "NVDA plus t". Once the answer has begun, Control stops the rest, as it stops a description; a key you press before the first sentence, NVDA+Alt+P too, does not throw the answer away. The whole answer is kept: once VisionClaw has finished, press NVDA+Alt+P to hear all of it again, or read it under Last task in the Ask VisionClaw window (NVDA+Alt+C).

It is spoken whole anyway when the answer is only what the tools did, which is ready at once; with the very smallest models, which quote what was looked up instead of wording it; when the model wrote a plan of its own, whose result is checked before you hear it, because the check may change it; and after "no, I meant ...", when a question may follow the answer and its window would silence NVDA.

Your choice: in VisionClaw's settings, Speak answers and descriptions a sentence at a time while the model writes them. It is on by default. Turn it off if speech stutters or pauses between sentences on a slow computer, where the model and the speech share the processor: then you hear each answer and description whole, once the model has finished. While you wait, the soft ticks and the status updates go on until it is spoken.

Asking by voice

Press NVDA+Alt+V, or Alt+V in the Ask VisionClaw window, and speak after the high beep. VisionClaw stops listening when you stop speaking; a lower beep means it is working out your words. Nothing is spoken while the microphone is open. What it heard is put into the box and read to you. Press Enter to send it, or correct it first: nothing is done before you send it.

Your words are turned into text on this computer by the speech model Qwen3-ASR and are sent nowhere; no recording is written to the disk. The first time, VisionClaw asks whether to download that model, about 1 GB, from Hugging Face. A larger one can be chosen in the settings. Ask "can I ask you by voice?" to hear whether it is set up and which speech model listens, "download the speech model" to get it now, and "delete the speech model" to remove it.

The speech model is told the names you are likely to say: the program in front, the words you taught VisionClaw, the focused control, the window's title and a few of its controls. A name it heard split in two, such as "wave table" for Wavetable, is joined again. Measured with Windows' own voices on requests that name things in programs: 53 of 54 heard rightly, where 36 before.

Requests that follow the one before

After a request you can say: do that again; a bit more; the other way, too much or too fast; say that again; the same in Word; and the pitch?; open the second one. VisionClaw writes such a request out in full and tells you how it understood you: the answer begins "Understood as". Everything that changes something still asks first. What the request before was is kept only while NVDA runs.

Several things in one request, and things that depend on something

Say up to eight things in one request, in the order you want them done: "Go into my Windows settings, turn down my system volume, close Windows settings, then go to gmail.com in my Google Chrome and tell me how many emails I have." Each is announced as a step of its own.

A thing that depends on something is checked first: "if you see the Recycle Bin, open it", "if Notepad is open, close it", "if my recycle bin is not empty, empty it". VisionClaw tells you what it found, and when the rest was not needed it says "So this was not done".

NVDA's settings and commands in plain words

Describe a setting and how you want it: "make NVDA speak a little faster", "set punctuation to all", "turn off the typing echo". Describe a command and VisionClaw runs it: "read the status bar", "what time is it", "what is the date today". A command that does something else on a second press, such as NVDA+F12 for the time and twice for the date, is pressed as often as your words ask. Ask "which key reads the title?" or "what are the keys for browse mode?" to be told the keys. VisionClaw tells you the keys of what it ran, so that you can do it yourself next time. When no command fits, it says so and runs nothing.

Your settings stay yours

VisionClaw changes an NVDA setting only when you ask it to, and only the setting you asked about. A change to a setting your request says nothing about is refused. Every change is written down. Ask "what did you change?" to hear the changes, and "undo that" or "put the speech rate back" to put a setting back to what it was. Installing or updating VisionClaw changes no NVDA setting.

A setting only for one program

Say what you want and add where: "make NVDA speak a little faster, only in this program", "turn off the typing echo just in Notepad", "when I'm in Word, set punctuation to all". NVDA keeps such a setting in a configuration profile, which it turns on by itself whenever that program is in front. Where the program has none yet, VisionClaw says so and always asks before it makes one. The setting is changed only while that program is in front; other programs stay as they were. Say "undo that" while you are in that program to put it back. "Which programs have their own settings?" lists them, and "remove the settings for this program" deletes the program's profile after a question. NVDA's own window for profiles is NVDA+Control+P.

Everywhere, or only here

Say "everywhere" or "in every application" to change NVDA's normal setting for all programs, also while the program in front has settings of its own. "Stop announcing capital letters in Word" is enough to mean Word. Where the program in front has settings of its own and you say neither, VisionClaw asks: only in this program, or everywhere? After a change it names the programs that keep a value of their own for that setting.

The control you are on

Ask about the control you are on in your own words, and VisionClaw answers at once from what NVDA knows, without asking you what you meant: "what is the focused control", "what does this button do", "what happens if I press Enter here", "is this checkbox checked", "is this button greyed out", "does this have a shortcut key", "what is the navigator object on", "what's under my mouse". In browse mode it is the link, button or field at your reading position.

The answer starts with what you asked: yes or no for a state, then its kind and name, the window and the program, its state (checked, pressed, expanded, unavailable), its value or selected text, its place in its list, what it is inside of, its own description and shortcut key, how it is used from the keyboard, and NVDA's own key that says it, for next time (NVDA+Tab for the focus). A control without a name is given the name you or VisionClaw saved for it; if there is none, the model that sees reads one from its picture, said with "probably" where it is not sure. Asked what a button does where nothing says it, the model guesses from its name and picture, and the answer says it is a guess. Nothing is pressed or changed. "Tell me everything NVDA knows about this control" still gives every technical fact.

Questions: NVDA, the program, the window, Windows

A question such as "how do I", "what key", "what is" or "what does this key do" is asked of four places, every time, in this order:

  1. NVDA: every command that works where you are, with its keys; all of NVDA's user guide; the help of your add-ons, this page among them.
  2. The program in front: which program it is, its version, NVDA's commands for it, and the hotkeys VisionClaw made for it.
  3. Its window: the title, the control that has the focus with its state, its place in its list, its own key and what it is inside of, the status bar, and the controls that have keys of their own.
  4. Windows: which Windows this is, which windows are open, and the keys of Windows: the desktop, the Start menu, the taskbar, switching and closing windows, File Explorer, menus, dialogs, text, copy and paste.

After these come your library, what you asked VisionClaw to remember, and the notes on programs. Plain code then keeps only what is about your question, and the answer says where it is from. When nothing is about your question, VisionClaw says so and names what it asked; it does not give you the nearest thing. Ask "what can I do here?" or "where am I?" to hear the program, the window and the control you are on. "What is new in NVDA" is answered from NVDA's own list of what is new, for its newest three versions. Nothing is looked up online.

Learning about the program or game you are in

Ask "learn about this program" or "learn about this game" while you are in it, or name one: "learn about Audacity". VisionClaw finds out which program it is, a model says what it is called and what kind of thing it is, and you are asked before anything goes online. Then it reads what Wikipedia says about it, asks Wikidata for the address of the program's own website and manual, and reads a few help pages from that website.

What it learns is kept as short notes in a Markdown file on your computer, one for each program, in the folder notes of VisionClaw's data. The model writes the notes and plain code keeps only those whose keys and words are in the page. Every note says which page it is from. The notes may be wrong; they are from the internet.

  • Then ask your questions in the program: how do I duplicate a line, which key closes a tab. An answer from the notes begins "From the notes on".
  • Which notes do you have? Open the notes on this program. Delete the notes on this program. Delete all notes.
  • Add this page to the notes on Notepad++: adds the page open in your own browser. Nothing is fetched for it.

VisionClaw itself asks no search engine: they turn programs away, and VisionClaw does not work around that. A program Wikipedia does not know is not found; search for it in your own browser, open a page, and add that page.

Working the computer for you

  • Opening things by name: "open the Recycle Bin", "open Notepad", "go into my Windows settings", "open Bluetooth settings", "open my Downloads folder". Programs of the Start menu, things on your desktop, folders, tools and settings pages of Windows, and your own files.
  • Parts of Windows itself: "open the Start menu", "click the Start button", "go to the system tray", "open quick settings", "go to the taskbar", "open the notification center", "open Task View", "open the Run dialog", "open clipboard history", "open the emoji panel". VisionClaw opens it with Windows' own key, tells you that key for next time, and how to move inside it. These are in no program's window. Where a program could have a Start button of its own and nothing in its window is called Start, you are asked whether the Start menu of Windows is meant.
  • What is in a part of Windows itself: "what is in my system tray", "tell me all objects that are currently in the system tray", "is NVDA running in the system tray", "how many icons are in the system tray", "which programs are on my taskbar", "what icons are on my desktop", "read my notifications", "what is in quick settings". VisionClaw reads it from what Windows tells screen readers, word for word, with no model and no picture, and says the key that goes there. The hidden tray icons are read too: VisionClaw shows them for a moment through Windows itself and hides them again, and the window you were in comes back to the front. The notifications, quick settings, the Start menu and clipboard history exist only while they are open, so VisionClaw opens them with their own key, reads them, and leaves them open; Escape closes them.
  • Something inside the system tray or on the taskbar is clicked there: "right-click the volume icon in the system tray", "click the Firefox button on the taskbar". And when you ask to click something that is in no window, such as "click Dropbox", VisionClaw looks for it in the notification area and on the taskbar and asks before it uses it there.
  • When what you ask to click is not in the window in front: if it is a program, folder or place of Windows, VisionClaw offers to open it by its name. When the item a model picked has none of the words you said, you are asked before anything is clicked, and when the model says it is not there, nothing is clicked.
  • The Recycle Bin: "check and see if my recycle bin needs to be emptied" says how many items it holds, how much room they take and whether emptying is worth it. "Empty the recycle bin" always asks first, because what is emptied cannot be brought back, and counts again afterwards.
  • The computer's own volume: "turn down my system volume", "set the computer's volume to 40 percent". The level is read back to you. Turning it down stops at 10 percent so that NVDA can still be heard, and muting is refused when no braille display is in use. A bare "volume" means NVDA's own volume.
  • Windows: "switch to Firefox", "close Notepad", "close Windows settings", "minimize all windows", "maximize this window". A window that is still open after it was asked to close is said to be open.
  • Your folders: "what is in my downloads folder", "find the file named budget".
  • Facts about the computer: "how much disk space do I have left", "which programs are running", "am I connected to the internet", "how long has the computer been on".
  • "Lock my computer", "restart my computer" and "shut down the computer" always ask first, and a restart or shutdown can be called off for 30 seconds: say "call off the shutdown".

Your own files and folders

"Create a folder called Taxes in my documents", "create a text file called shopping list on my desktop", "rename the file budget to Budget 2026", "move the file report.pdf from my downloads to my documents", "copy the file notes to my desktop", "delete the file old notes". VisionClaw looks for the file by its name in your Desktop, Documents, Downloads, Pictures, Music and Videos folders, and nowhere else. When several files fit the name, it asks which one you mean. It always tells you what it is about to do and asks first.

Nothing is ever overwritten: when something with that name is already there, VisionClaw says so and changes nothing. Deleting means moving to the Recycle Bin, from where the file can be brought back; it is always asked about. "Undo that" puts a renamed or moved file back, and removes a folder or file VisionClaw made while it is still empty. "What did you change" lists these with the settings it changed.

Options inside a program

"Find the settings button in this app, then turn the volume up a bit and turn the equalizer down", "in this dialog, turn on my NVIDIA GPU for this program". The control is looked for by its name in the window, on the other pages of its dialog, and in the window's picture where NVDA cannot read it. It is worked the least risky way that works: its own action, then the keyboard, then the mouse. You are asked first and told what it was and what it is now; a reading from the picture is said to be one.

Working through a task when the usual path isn't clear

Say what you want and that you are stuck: "I'm stuck, I want to attach a file to this email", "walk me through exporting this as a PDF". VisionClaw finds the thing in the program that leads there, tells you what it is and its key, and asks whether to press it, only tell you where it is, or stop. It asks before every step, checks in after three, stays in this one program, and tells you what each press brought up. VisionClaw's own windows are off limits to it: it never clicks, types or presses keys in them. Their controls are yours.

A date in a calendar that takes no keys

Have the calendar showing and say: "pick 14 March 2027 in this calendar", or "choose next Friday in the date picker". VisionClaw asks, presses the calendar's own button for the next or previous month until the right month shows, then presses the day, and tells you the date with its weekday and what it did. A date that could be read two ways is put to you. A calendar that is only drawn is read from the window's picture; then check the date field afterwards.

  • NVDA+Alt+N: give your own name to the object where you are, or to an overlay item; twice for the saved names.

Press a describe command once for a quick spoken description, twice for a detailed description in a window where you can ask questions about the image. Press Control to stop speaking. On a photo message in a chat app, or an image file in File Explorer, NVDA+Alt+A describes the photo itself. You can also ask in words: "what is on my screen?", or "read the text in this window" for the exact text shown.

A question about what is on the screen, asked in words, is put to the model that sees in its separate parts: "what is in this photo, and is there any text in it?" is two questions, asked one after the other with the same picture. Each gets the one rule of its kind: text is read exactly as written, a count gives the number first, "is there" is answered yes or no first and then where, a colour is named in plain words, people are described without saying who they are, a chart is told by its title, axes and trend. You hear each answer after its own question.

In the description window every question is answered: what is highlighted or selected, where something is, what a thing is for, what could be done next. What the picture does not show, the model says is not shown. A question about a key, such as "how do I read the title of this window", is given NVDA's real command for it, so the key you hear is NVDA's own.

Object names

Many programs, games and web pages have buttons, pictures and other controls without a name, or with a name that says nothing: NVDA then says only "button", "graphic", a file name such as icon_close.svg, or the sign of an icon font. Press NVDA+Alt+B and VisionClaw says "Object names on". From then on every object that is truly unlabelled, with no label from Windows, the browser or the program, is named by the local model that sees, the moment you reach it, and the name is saved. Press NVDA+Alt+B again and it says "Object names off". These are accessibility fixes made on the fly, for the times you just want a control to say what it is: nothing is written, installed or asked. When an object is not named and you want a name for it anyway, press NVDA+Shift+Alt+B.

  • However you move: Tab, Shift+Tab, Control+Tab and the arrow keys in lists and menus; in browse mode the arrow keys, the quick navigation keys such as B for the next button and G for the next graphic, and NVDA+F7's elements list, without leaving browse mode; object navigation, such as NVDA+Numpad 6 or NVDA+Shift+Right Arrow on a laptop; the review cursor; and the mouse. NVDA asks the object for its name, and VisionClaw answers with the saved one.
  • Only what is truly unlabelled is named: whatever Windows, the browser or the program gives NVDA is a label, however short or odd, and is left as it is: one letter or number, such as a page number or X; a sign such as + or an emoji; a file name in a list of files; a name from the program's code, such as btnClose. The label the program gives in another way counts too: a description or tooltip, the hint written in an empty field, the words inside a button, and the text the program says labels a field. Where NVDA would not say such a label by itself, VisionClaw says it, as it is, without the model: the label next to a field that the program links to it, and the words inside a button. Truly unlabelled is: no name at all; only the signs of an icon font or characters that are never printed; only the word for its kind, such as button on a button; a word a program gives by a mistake, such as undefined; and on a picture, its file name, a web address or a word that only says it is a picture. An empty table cell, an empty list and a pane that holds other objects are not named, and nor is a picture inside a button or link: the button or link is named instead, where it needs a name.
  • Ask for a name yourself: NVDA+Shift+Alt+B asks the model for a name for the object where you are, in browse mode for the control or picture at the cursor: where object names left it alone because it has a label you do not understand, such as X, where the model could not name it before, and also while object names are off. The beep says the name is on its way, then you hear it, with probably where the model is not sure, or that the model could not tell. It is saved, and said there from then on, over the program's own label, while object names are on; forget it in the window of saved names.
  • When a name for the object you are on is on its way, you hear a short, soft, high beep, then the name. On a slower computer this tells you to wait a moment. The beep goes with the setting "Play a soft tick while waiting for a description": turn that off and this beep is off too.
  • A name the model is not sure of is said with probably before it: "probably Settings button". A name is sure when the text written on the object, read exactly with Windows OCR, or what the program's own code calls it, agrees with the model. Your own names, and names a person shared, are said as they are.
  • Names are kept for each program and each website, in the file labels.json of VisionClaw's data folder, and NVDA says them from then on, in speech and braille. VisionClaw's tools and its models see them too, and a name found in a program's code is added to VisionClaw's notes on that program or website. On a web page the controls a little further down, and in a program the objects next to the one you reach, are named in the background, so that their names are ready when you get there.
  • NVDA+Alt+N gives your own name to the object where you are: in browse mode the control or picture at the cursor, elsewhere the object at the review position, or the current item of the overlay. Leave the name empty to take your name away. Your own name always wins, also over the program's own label. You can also ask: "call this button play".
  • Press NVDA+Alt+N twice, or the button "Saved object names: change, forget or share them" in VisionClaw's settings, for the window of saved object names: the programs and websites with names, the names of the one you choose with who gave each and how sure it is, and the buttons Change the selected name, Forget checked names, Forget all names of this place, Share names and Add shared names.
  • Share names saves the names of the programs and websites you check to a file. Give it to another VisionClaw user, who adds it with Add shared names. The file is plain data and names those programs and websites, nothing else about you. Every line is checked before it is used, and a shared name never replaces your own; it replaces only a guess of the model. A name kept by what the object's code calls it fits on every computer; one kept by its picture fits only where the object looks exactly the same.
  • The first time you turn object names on, VisionClaw says once that, because these are fixes made on the fly, a small model, 7B or smaller, is best for them: it names an object quickly. If the model you use is larger, it is named. You choose the model in the models window, NVDA+Alt+M; nothing is changed for you.

What object names cannot do: an object that is not on the screen, or while the screen curtain is on, has no picture and is not named. A page that NVDA reads through UI Automation, such as a Word document, does not get names in its text yet. The model can be wrong, which is why a name it is not sure of has probably. Text and headings are read from their own words and are never named.

Making a website or a program accessible, for good

Ask "do a deep inspection of this website", "inspect this window for accessibility problems" or "what is wrong with this button". VisionClaw reads every control NVDA knows there, watches for a moment to see what changes by itself, compares the picture with the text, and tells you in plain numbered sentences what is in the way and which of it an add-on can fix. Inspecting changes nothing.

Then ask "fix all of it" or "fix number 2", or say what you want: "give this site keys to move between posts", "make NVDA+Alt+P press the Play button on this page", "make a key that reads the time", "name the focused button Share on this website", "tell me when the inbox count changes". You are told exactly what will be made and asked before it is turned on. The fixes for one website, in any browser, or for one program are kept in one add-on named fixes and then the site or program. It stays until you say "remove the add-on" and its name, and "save the add-on ... as a file" gives you a file to install for good or to share. Outside that website or program its keys do what they always do.

No model writes this add-on's code: it is put together from fixed, tested parts and the list of your fixes. A control that nothing in the page's code tells apart from others cannot be named reliably, and the report says how many those are. A name taken from a button's picture is a guess, and you are told so.

Names from pictures: the model that sees answers in one to three words that say what the control does, never its colour or shape. A larger model is also told the program, the window and the named controls around the control; a small one is not, because it copied those names. A state that may be missing: the report says when tabs report none of them as selected, and when buttons that open or show something do not say whether it is open. Then ask "which tab is selected" or "what is open here": the model that sees reads it from the picture, and says so.

What VisionClaw remembers about the place: after a deep check, how each control is worked, names read from pictures, what drew the window and what the check found are kept in a Markdown file for that program or website, in the folder access of VisionClaw's data folder. A control the models agreed on before is not put to them again. When VisionClaw writes an add-on for that program, the lines that fit go with the request. The file is yours to read, change or delete. Where the picture and what is reported still disagree, VisionClaw offers the model for hard cases, if it is on your computer.

Working the mouse from the keyboard

Ask VisionClaw to right-click, double-click or point at an item, or to drag one: "right-click the file icon", "drag the file onto the Trash", "drag the volume slider to the right a little". Ask it to scroll: "scroll down", "scroll down a page", "scroll this window to the bottom". It turns the mouse wheel, compares the window's picture before and after, and tells you whether anything moved and, where a scrollbar shows it, how far along the view stands.

Every key of pointer mode and of the overlay is one that every keyboard has: letters, the arrow keys, Enter, Space, Escape, Shift and Control. Nothing depends on an Applications key, Page Up, Page Down, Home, End or a function key. Where your keyboard has them they still work as before.

Pointer mode, NVDA+Alt+K, or "make this control keyboard navigable", is for a control, canvas or game where nothing can be listed. Its keys:

  • The arrow keys move the mouse pointer in the window: one step, with Shift a fine step, with Control a far one. The steps follow your screen's size and scaling. A short tone tells you how high the pointer is.
  • Enter or Space clicks, Shift+Enter double-clicks, A right-clicks.
  • G holds the mouse button down and G again lets go, for dragging.
  • I turns the mouse wheel up and K down, J left and L right, about a page; with Shift one notch.
  • S finds the scrollbars and puts the pointer on one.
  • W says what is at the pointer, R reads the text there, P says where the pointer is, B brings it back to the middle of the window.
  • T rests the pointer and says what appears. C clicks the moment what is under the pointer changes; Shift+C only sounds a tone then.
  • D tries what is under the pointer as a dial or slider.
  • M changes how the mouse is moved, for a game that reads the mouse directly.
  • H repeats the keys, and Escape turns pointer mode off.

A click or a turn of the wheel waits until you have let go of Shift, Control and Alt, because held down they change what the mouse does in most programs; if they stay down, nothing is done and you are told.

Dials, knobs and sliders that are only worked by dragging

Some programs, music tools and games have a dial, a knob or a slider of their own that takes no keys. Ask "let the arrow keys work the volume dial" or "give me a hotkey for the gain knob". VisionClaw asks first, then finds out by itself how the control is worked: through Windows itself where the program offers its value, else with the mouse wheel, else by dragging it up and down, sideways, or around in a circle. It tries each one step and puts it back. From then on, until you press Escape:

  • Up and Right turn it up, Down and Left turn it down; with Shift a small step, with Control a large one.
  • T goes to its top end and B to its bottom end.
  • After every press you hear what changed: the new text at the control, or "Up", or that nothing moved.
  • R reads the text at it, W asks the vision model what it shows, M tries another way of working it, H repeats the keys.
  • K keeps it under a hotkey for this program.

Where nothing can be named, as in many games: press NVDA+Alt+K for pointer mode, move the pointer onto the control, and press D.

Games that read the mouse directly

Many games take no notice of where the mouse pointer is put. Tell VisionClaw once which kind it is: "the mouse turns the view in this game", or "this game has its own mouse pointer". It asks and keeps that for the game.

  • A view the mouse turns: "turn left", "look up a little", "turn around". VisionClaw moves the mouse itself and says whether the view changed. "Direct mouse movement is too fast" or "too slow" changes how far, and is kept for the game. How far a game turns is a guess until you say so.
  • A pointer the game draws itself: clicking, holding and dragging things by name work again. VisionClaw pushes the game's pointer into the corner of the window, measures how fast it moves, and counts from there.
  • In pointer mode, M changes between Windows' pointer, the game's own pointer and a view that the mouse turns. For a view the arrow keys move the mouse itself and Enter clicks where the game aims.

A game that refuses movements made by a program cannot be worked this way.

A game controller

VisionClaw notices a connected game controller and says so. Hold the controller's View and Menu buttons together for a second, or press NVDA+Alt+J, for controller mode; the View button turns it off. Out of controller mode the controller is left alone.

  • The left stick moves the mouse pointer: a small push slowly, a full push fast. When it comes to rest you hear what is under the pointer. The direction pad moves it one step.
  • A clicks, B right-clicks, X double-clicks, Y says what is under the pointer.
  • The right trigger is the mouse button for as long as you pull it: for dragging, and for holding something down. The left trigger presses like a pen, as hard as you pull it.
  • The right stick turns the mouse wheel. Pressed in, it moves the mouse itself instead, for a game that reads the mouse directly.
  • With the left shoulder button held, the stick is slow and exact; A clicks the moment what is under the pointer changes, B rests the pointer and says what appears, X tries what is there as a dial or slider, Y reads the text there.
  • With the right shoulder button held, the stick is fast; right and left on the direction pad jump between the things found in the window and say their names; Y has the picture at the pointer described.
  • The Menu button says all of this.

A button can also be a hotkey of a program: "make the X button on my controller click Play". Ask "is a controller connected?" or "what do the buttons on my controller do?" at any time. Whether a connected controller is announced is your choice in the settings. VisionClaw cannot press a controller's buttons for you, and the buzz is never the only sign: everything controller mode does is spoken.

VisionClaw never installs, removes, turns on or turns off an add-on.

NVDA Coach is a free add-on by Tony Gebhard, in NVDA's Add-on Store, that teaches NVDA in lessons of single steps. VisionClaw works with it only when you ask to be coached or taught, and gives as much help as you ask for. "Give me a hint about browse mode" gives one hint and no more. "Coach me on reading the current line" gives the one step about it. "Teach me object navigation step by step" gives the lesson's steps in order. "Which lessons does NVDA Coach have" and "how far am I in NVDA Coach" tell you the chapters and what you have finished. "Open NVDA Coach" opens its own window. Without NVDA Coach, "teach me how to" is answered from NVDA's own user guide. Say "from now on, give me only a hint" or "always explain things step by step", and VisionClaw remembers how much help you want.

Which version is installed

Ask "which version of VisionClaw do I have", "what version of NVDA am I running", "what version of Access8Math is installed" with any add-on's name, or "what version of this program is it". VisionClaw says that version and nothing more: "VisionClaw is version 1.15.1." Ask about several at once, "what versions of NVDA and VisionClaw do I have", and you hear one line for each. An add-on that is turned off is said to be; an add-on you name that is not installed is said not to be, with the name of the program in front.

Only when you say you are new to this ("I'm a beginner screen reader user, ...", "I'm new to NVDA"), or asked VisionClaw to remember that you are, does the answer add where to find the version yourself next time: for NVDA, NVDA+N, then H for Help, and About; for an add-on, NVDA+N, then T for Tools, then A for the Add-on Store, whose Installed add-ons tab lists every add-on with its version; for VisionClaw, NVDA+Alt+U to look for a newer one.

The version is read from NVDA itself, from the add-on's own manifest and from what the program tells NVDA, by plain code: no model is asked and nothing goes online. "Is there a newer version of VisionClaw" still looks on GitHub, after a question.

Add-ons written by VisionClaw

Ask "write an add-on that beeps when a link gets the focus". You never have to read, write or debug code. The coder model writes the add-on, the code is checked and compared with what you asked for, the other model reviews it, and you are always asked whether to turn it on, after being told in plain words what checking the code shows: the keys it adds, what it reacts to, which programs it works in, and whether it speaks, beeps, presses keys or clicks.

When an add-on VisionClaw wrote fails, NVDA keeps working. The add-on is paused, you hear that it is being corrected, the models debug it from the exact error, and the corrected add-on is loaded again. If that does not hold after three tries it is turned off and you are told; "fix the add-on" followed by its name lets you say what it does wrong. Generated add-ons start without restarting NVDA and come back after a restart. Ask "list what you have made" or "remove the add-on" followed by its name to manage them. Ask "save the add-on" followed by its name "as a file" to get a normal add-on file in your Documents folder, under VisionClaw add-ons, that you can install for good or share.

An add-on is written piece by piece. Ask "write an add-on that beeps when a link gets the focus, says the time with NVDA+Alt+T, and only works in Firefox": plain program code reads it into pieces, one key command or one thing that happens by itself each, and writes everything around them: the key, the event, the kind of control, the state ("gets checked" speaks only when the box is now checked), the program, and a beep or a fixed sentence. The model writes only the few lines inside one piece, fed from that piece's section of the work sheet with the notes that fit that piece, and never sees the other pieces. Each piece is checked and reviewed on its own, and the whole add-on once more. A key command whose key you did not name gets a key nothing else uses, and you are told which. You are told what it cannot do, such as a heading in browse mode, which never gets the focus. What cannot be read into pieces, such as something every hour or at NVDA's start, is written as one piece as before, and you are told why. When an add-on fails in one place, only the method where it failed is corrected.

The check prevents accidents; it is not a sandbox. Once you say yes, the code runs inside NVDA like any other add-on. Small models make mistakes.

Skills

A skill tells VisionClaw how a kind of job is done well for a blind person: describing a photo, web forms, email, braille, games, NVDA for a beginner, stubborn controls, and many more; 66 come with VisionClaw. VisionClaw chooses skills from the words of your request, at most two at a time. A skill never holds code, and every change is still asked about.

Make your own in words: "make a new skill: when I ask about recipes, read the ingredients first". Ask "which skills do you have", "what does the email skill say", "turn off the game skill", or "delete my recipes skill". The skills window, NVDA+Alt+I, or the button in the settings, lists every skill and what it says, with the buttons New skill, Change, Turn off or on, Delete, Import a skill file, Open the skills folder, and Read the skills again. A skill that comes with VisionClaw is never deleted, only turned off; a changed one is kept as your copy, and deleting your copy brings back the original.

Memories

Say "remember that I use a braille display with 40 cells". VisionClaw keeps your own words as a small Markdown file, one for each memory, in the folder memories of its data, and uses them with later requests. It also keeps something lasting you say about how you work the computer when nothing else kept it, and says so and how to undo it; the setting "Remember things" decides this. Ask "what do you remember", "forget that", or "forget all", which is always asked about. The memories window, from the button in the settings, has a check box for each memory, the whole memory to read, Forget checked, and Forget all and start over. How fast you like answers is kept without being asked: "the 9B is too slow for me", "I like quick answers", "accuracy matters most to me"; the model VisionClaw picks or suggests follows it. Memories are found by what they mean: a memory about the system tray is found when you ask about the notification area.

Your library

Put Markdown, text, saved web pages or Word documents into VisionClaw's library folder, and questions are answered from them too, after NVDA, the program, its window and Windows. A document whose name names a program counts for that program. Ask "what is in my library" or use the button in the settings to open the folder.

How VisionClaw answers, and your own instructions

Every answer, every description of a picture and every add-on written on the fly is told who you are: blind, listening to speech or reading braille. Answers start with the answer, use no Markdown, copy names, numbers and keys exactly, say places in words, and say what cannot be read instead of guessing. In the settings, the button "How VisionClaw answers, and your own instructions" lets you read and rewrite every instruction, add your own, and restore the original of one or all of them. They apply to whatever model you use.

Nothing VisionClaw says or shows you is cut, however long: the answer, every question it asks you, every "You asked" before a change, a list you asked for, and what each step found when you read back the last request in the Ask window. Your own request reaches every model and every tool whole, and so does what you ask VisionClaw to remember. VisionClaw's log keeps every entry whole. Only what a small model reads of looked-up pages, notes and results is kept short for it, so that it is not swamped; you never hear that part shortened.

Where a model words an answer, plain code still checks two things before you hear it. A key it spelled out, "NVDA plus t", is written as NVDA writes it, NVDA+t, so that a braille display shows it as NVDA's own lists do. Words from VisionClaw's question to the model, such as "the information provided", are taken out or said as what they are: what was looked up. Ask "how do I hear the window title" with NVDA+Alt+C to hear it.

You have it without a download. The NVDA Add-on Development Guide does not come with it, because its authors have not said that it may be passed on.

Free training material, and everything as Markdown files

Finding out what VisionClaw can download for you

Ask "what can you download for me", or press the button "What VisionClaw can download for you" in VisionClaw's settings. VisionClaw names everything it can get from the internet for you, says which of it is on your computer already, and gives the words that get each. It also tells you by itself, in one sentence after a fitting answer that begins "This is said once": after you ask for an add-on, that the guide for writing add-ons can be downloaded; after you ask to be taught something, that lessons can be. Each of these sentences is said once and never again, and nothing is downloaded until you ask and say yes.

How VisionClaw plans, checks and learns

Every request is planned in small steps, and plain code helps the model at every step:

  • Where a request is about is read from your words: the window in front, a web page, or a part of Windows itself such as the system tray, the taskbar or the desktop. Each place is read in the way that can really reach it.
  • What was found is checked by plain code. When a way of looking did not find what you asked, as when a picture of the desktop "cannot see the system tray", VisionClaw does not stop there: it tries the next way that only looks, once, tells you so, and says what that way found. Nothing is changed by the next way, so nothing is asked.
  • VisionClaw learns from it on this computer: the way that found nothing is counted as not working for that request, and the way that answered as working, in plans.json in VisionClaw's data folder.
  • Words that mean the same thing count as one, so what was learned for one wording is used for another. "Tell me all objects in the system tray" and "which icons are in my notification area" are the same request to VisionClaw. It is a short table of words, all about working the computer with the keyboard, speech and braille, and a model still chooses and plain code still checks.
  • A question about how to do something is answered with the keys, and nothing is done; a question that begins with why is answered. When the model's choice also fits your words, VisionClaw goes with it instead of asking you; it asks only when your words clearly point elsewhere and one of the two ways would change something. Between two ways that only look it does not ask: it tries one, then the other if the first finds nothing.
  • How well VisionClaw chooses is measured on sets of requests in words it was never tuned on, with the real model, and a change is kept only when it measures better.

The models

Ask "what model is installed", "which model are you using" or "how big is the model". The answer is read from your settings and your disk, not made up by a model: which model does which job, what is stored, how big it is, and where a loaded model runs.

  • Every model is offered whatever your computer is, the 7B and 9B ones too. VisionClaw suggests a model; you decide.
  • Every model can be chosen as Q4_K_M, smaller and faster, or Q6_K, larger and more exact, in a second list under each model, with its size. Q4_K_M is suggested.
  • The models window has a button, "Look for newer models". Only when you press it and say yes, VisionClaw reads the list of offered Qwen models from its own page on GitHub, so newer small models can be offered without a new version of the add-on.
  • NVDA+Alt+M, or "See, remove or swap models" in the settings: remove some or all models and choose others. You are always asked before anything is deleted, and a removed model can be downloaded again. After you choose other models, VisionClaw offers to remove the ones you no longer use.
  • Which model for what you want to do: "which model should I use for describing pictures", "should I switch to a smaller model", "which model is best for writing add-ons", "suggest a model". VisionClaw suggests one from your graphics card and memory, what was measured with each model and on which day, and your own review: how your requests went with each model on this computer, kept in models-went.json, and what you asked it to remember about a model. No model is asked, nothing goes online, and nothing is changed: you choose in the models window, NVDA+Alt+M. Ask too about "stubborn programs", "the model for hard cases" or "which speech model should I use": the answer also says whether your graphics card has room for the Q6_K of the suggested model, whether the model for hard cases runs on your computer and how, and that the speech models come only as Q8_0.
  • In the models window, the button "Suggest models for what I want to do": check what you want VisionClaw for with Space (planning, pictures, add-ons, quick answers, stubborn programs, hard cases, asking by voice) and press Enter. NVDA says each choice as a check box, checked or not checked, and says it again when Space changes it. The reasons are put into the box at the top and the suggested models into the lists. Nothing is downloaded until you press the first button.
  • Let VisionClaw pick the model for each job. In VisionClaw's settings, "Which model does each job" is at first "The models I chose in the models window; VisionClaw only suggests". Choose "VisionClaw picks from the models on this computer for each request, without asking", and before each request VisionClaw picks among the models you have downloaded, by plain code: for planning and pictures the largest that fits your graphics card with room to spare, or with no card that holds one the largest that fits your memory; for writing an add-on the largest model for code that fits beside it, or the same model when it writes code too, so that nothing more is loaded; a faster one only when you said you want speed, such as "the 9B is too slow for me", and the request is not a hard one and has no picture or code in it. A model with which your requests went badly here is passed over while another can do the job. It never downloads anything and never picks the model for hard cases. Nothing is said while it picks: press NVDA+Alt+P to hear what it picked, or ask "which model are you using". With your own choice kept, that question also tells you which models on this computer VisionClaw would pick, only where they differ from yours; it is never said by itself.
  • A small model is asked a choice more than once. When Qwen3.5 2B, 0.8B or Qwen2.5 Coder 1.5B chooses among a few fixed answers, such as what kind of request this is, it is asked three times and the answer most of them gave is taken; larger models are asked once. Measured with the real 2B and 0.8B choosing the job: on 202 requests with the 2B one more was right and one question fewer, none worse, and choosing took a third longer; with the 0.8B nothing changed.
  • The model for hard cases. The everyday models are 9B or smaller. Qwen3 14B, in Q4_K_M or Q6_K, is kept for what they could not do: an add-on that does not pass VisionClaw's checks, will not start, or fails again after it was corrected, and a control of a stubborn program whose picture and what is reported about it say different things. Then VisionClaw asks whether to try once more with it. Choose it in the models window, in the list "Model for hard cases" under the coder lists; None is chosen at first, so nothing of 9 GB is downloaded unasked. Uncheck "Ask me before the model for hard cases is used" to be asked no more, or say "fix my add-on Beeper with the model for hard cases" to use it at once. It is slower, the everyday models come back with the next request, NVDA+Alt+X stops it, and it reads and writes text only: no Qwen model of that size sees pictures. Measured in Q4_K_M on an 8 GB card shared with the processor: of nine add-ons written piece by piece, all nine did what was asked, where those of Qwen3.5 9B did eight; of twelve controls a program draws itself, it told six rightly, as the 9B did, with fewer wrong guesses.
  • The two very small models, Qwen3.5 0.8B and Qwen2.5 Coder 0.5B, are asked one small question at a time and answer a question about something looked up with a line of it, word for word. They are fast and less accurate.
  • Where the models run: a dedicated graphics card, Vulkan, or the processor. If the chosen place does not work, VisionClaw falls back by itself: dedicated card, then Vulkan, then the processor. Two large models that do not fit on one chip run side by side, the second on another chip or the processor. A model larger than the card is shared between the card and the processor, as much of it on the card as fits; a model that should fit but does not start whole is tried that way too before the card is given up.

VisionClaw's own log

VisionClaw keeps a log of its own, so that when something goes wrong it can be found out why; it does not depend on NVDA's logging level. Ask "open the VisionClaw log", or use the button in the settings, where you also choose Detailed, Brief or Off. Detailed, the default, records every request, step, question to a model and its answer, and every error with the place in the code. The log stays on your computer and is sent nowhere; it holds what you asked and what was read from your screen, so look through it before you give it to someone.

Taking VisionClaw along: a portable copy

Everything VisionClaw keeps is inside its own add-on folder, in the folder data: the models, the guides, the training material, notes, memories, skills, your library, the logs, and whatever else you have it keep. When VisionClaw is updated, this folder moves into the newer version by itself; when VisionClaw is removed, it is kept in NVDA's configuration folder, and a later install takes it in again.

In VisionClaw's settings, press "Make a portable copy of VisionClaw with everything in it" (Alt+X). The window says what goes in, part by part with its size, and offers two ways: one file that installs VisionClaw with everything on any computer, which NVDA asks about before it installs it; or VisionClaw with everything, straight into a portable NVDA, as on a flash drive. Nothing is left out of the data folder, and your VisionClaw settings come along. A button opens NVDA's own window for making a portable NVDA: check "Copy current user configuration" there, and VisionClaw comes along with everything. A progress bar shows how far the copy is, and Cancel stops it. A drive formatted as FAT32 cannot hold a file of 4 GB or more; VisionClaw says so before it starts.

Getting a newer version of VisionClaw

Press NVDA+Alt+U, use the button "Look for a newer version now" in VisionClaw's settings, or ask "check for a new version of VisionClaw". VisionClaw reads the list of its versions from its own page on GitHub. If there is a newer one, a window opens with the focus in a read-only edit box that holds its version, its size and what is new, one point to a line. Read it with the arrow keys as often as you like. Tab reaches two buttons, Download and install, and Not now; Escape means not now. The file is checked against the size and SHA-256 GitHub reports, which shows that it arrived whole, not who made it, and then NVDA itself asks whether to install it and restarts.

In the settings, "Look for a newer version of VisionClaw" can be set to "When NVDA starts, at most once a day, and tell me". Then you hear one sentence when a newer version exists; nothing is downloaded until you ask. It starts on "Only when I ask".

Requirements

  • Windows 10 or 11, 64-bit, and NVDA 2024.1 or later.
  • 4 GB of free disk space for the two suggested models, more for larger ones, and 8 GB of RAM or more.
  • A graphics chip that supports Vulkan makes it much faster.

What has been tested

VisionClaw is tested with plain Python, with stand-ins for NVDA, Windows, the mouse and the internet, and with the real models in the engine. Most of it has not yet been run inside NVDA. These are small models: they are right most of the time on simple requests and less often on complicated ones. Please double-check anything important and report what does not work at github.com/joshknnd1982/visionclaw.

Privacy

VisionClaw does not collect any information. Screens, photos, requests, generated code, memories, notes and the log never leave your computer. What you say when you ask by voice is turned into text on your own computer and is not kept or sent anywhere. VisionClaw's own code connects to the internet only:

  • to download the models you choose, and the speech model after a question, from Hugging Face;
  • when you press Look for newer models in the models window and say yes, to read the list of models from its own page on GitHub;
  • when you ask for the latest developer guides and say yes, to download those two guides;
  • when you ask for the free training material and say yes, to download those pages from the NVDA wiki, AFB and Hadley;
  • when you ask it to learn about a program and say yes, to read about it on Wikipedia, Wikidata and the program's own website, sending the program's name and nothing about you;
  • when you ask it to look for a newer version, to read the list of its own versions from GitHub, and by itself only if you turn that on in the settings.

None of these happens by itself. When you ask it to open or search the web, it hands the address to your own browser, which goes online as usual; a web search sends your search words to the search engine you chose.
